Part II

The Evolution of Statistical Computing: From S Language to R, The Philosophy Behind R Syntax, and Why It Changed Computational Statistics Forever

Ladies and Gentlemen,

Before we can fully appreciate the extraordinary strength of R syntax in computational algorithmic statistics, it is important that we take a step back in time. Every revolutionary innovation has a story. Every influential technology has a history. Understanding where R came from helps us understand why it was designed the way it is and why, after more than three decades, it continues to occupy a central position in statistics, data science, artificial intelligence, and scientific computing.

The story of R is not simply the story of another programming language. It is the story of how statisticians sought a better way to think, compute, communicate, and discover.

To appreciate this journey, we must first revisit the era before modern statistical software existed.

There was a time when statistical analysis required enormous patience. Researchers relied on logarithm tables, slide rules, printed statistical tables, and mechanical calculators. Even relatively simple analyses demanded hours or days of manual calculations.

Imagine estimating a multiple regression model involving ten explanatory variables without a computer.

Imagine computing eigenvalues of a covariance matrix manually.

Imagine estimating nonlinear models using iterative numerical methods entirely by hand.

These were everyday realities for statisticians only a few decades ago.

Many of the statistical methods we teach in classrooms today—linear regression, principal component analysis, discriminant analysis, maximum likelihood estimation, cluster analysis, Bayesian inference, and multivariate statistics—were originally developed in an era when computational resources were extremely limited.

Consequently, researchers often selected statistical methods based not only on theoretical suitability but also on computational feasibility.

In other words, some methods were avoided simply because they were too difficult to compute.

The arrival of computers transformed this landscape forever.

Suddenly, statistical ideas that had remained largely theoretical became practically applicable.

However, another challenge quickly emerged.

Computers could perform calculations.

But how should statisticians communicate mathematical reasoning to computers?

This question led to the development of statistical programming languages.

One of the earliest and most influential answers came from Bell Laboratories in the United States during the 1970s.

At Bell Labs, a remarkable team of statisticians and computer scientists, led by John Chambers and his colleagues, developed a programming language known simply as S.

The objective behind S was revolutionary.

Rather than forcing statisticians to adapt their thinking to the limitations of existing programming languages, S sought to create a language that naturally reflected statistical reasoning.

John Chambers once expressed a philosophy that continues to define modern statistical computing:

"To turn ideas into software, and software into knowledge."

That statement captures the very essence of computational statistics.

Programming should never be an obstacle to scientific thinking.

Instead, programming should become a natural extension of scientific reasoning.

S introduced several concepts that were revolutionary for its time.

Objects.

Functions.

Interactive computing.

Graphics.

Data structures specifically designed for statistical analysis.

Although these ideas may appear ordinary today, they represented a dramatic shift from earlier programming paradigms.

Statisticians no longer needed to write lengthy procedural programs for every analysis.

Instead, they could work interactively.

They could manipulate datasets directly.

They could visualize results immediately.

They could experiment.

They could revise.

They could discover.

Scientific computing became exploratory rather than merely computational.

Years later, another remarkable chapter unfolded.

In the early 1990s, two statisticians at the University of Auckland in New Zealand—Ross Ihaka and Robert Gentleman—recognized both the strengths and limitations of S.

While S had become a powerful statistical language, access to commercial implementations was often expensive.

Academic researchers, students, and scientists in developing countries faced financial barriers.

Knowledge should not have barriers.

Scientific computing should not be reserved only for institutions with large budgets.

Inspired by this belief, Ross Ihaka and Robert Gentleman embarked on an ambitious project.

Their goal was simple yet transformative:

Develop a free implementation inspired by the S language.

That project became R.

The first versions of R appeared in the early 1990s.

Few people could have imagined the global impact this decision would eventually produce.

What began as an academic experiment has grown into one of the world's most influential open-source software ecosystems.

Today, millions of users rely on R across universities, research institutes, hospitals, governments, financial institutions, technology companies, pharmaceutical organizations, agricultural research centres, and international organizations.

The remarkable growth of R illustrates an important lesson.

When knowledge is shared openly, innovation accelerates.

The success of R was never merely about software.

It was about community.

Thousands of volunteers from around the world began contributing packages.

Researchers developed new algorithms.

Universities incorporated R into their curricula.

Industry professionals adopted R for real-world applications.

Government agencies embraced reproducible statistical computing.

The language evolved continuously because the community evolved continuously.

Today, the Comprehensive R Archive Network, commonly known as CRAN, hosts tens of thousands of contributed packages covering virtually every area of scientific inquiry.

Whether your research involves epidemiology, econometrics, genomics, psychometrics, ecology, astronomy, sports analytics, finance, education, engineering, natural language processing, artificial intelligence, spatial statistics, or computational biology, there is almost certainly an R package designed specifically for your work.

This extraordinary ecosystem is one of R's greatest strengths.

However, the ecosystem alone does not explain R's enduring popularity.

The true foundation lies deeper.

It lies within the design philosophy of its syntax.

Programming languages differ not only in what they can accomplish but also in how they encourage programmers to think.

Some languages prioritize execution speed.

Others prioritize system programming.

Others focus on hardware interaction.

Others emphasize web development.

R prioritizes statistical reasoning.

This distinction is fundamental.

Consider a simple example.

Suppose we have a dataset containing examination scores for one thousand students.

A statistician wishes to calculate the average score.

In many programming languages, this requires explicit loops.

The programmer instructs the computer to examine each observation individually.

The algorithm becomes procedural.

Step one.

Initialize the total.

Step two.

Read the first value.

Step three.

Add the value.

Step four.

Move to the next observation.

Continue until all observations have been processed.

Finally, divide by the number of observations.

The programmer is essentially instructing the computer how to perform every individual action.

R approaches the problem differently.

The statistician simply writes:

mean(scores)

One line.

One idea.

One statistical concept.

Notice what has happened.

The programmer is no longer thinking about machine operations.

The programmer is thinking statistically.

This is one of the defining characteristics of R syntax.

It allows researchers to express statistical concepts directly rather than translating every idea into low-level computational procedures.

This philosophy dramatically reduces cognitive complexity.

Instead of worrying about implementation details, researchers concentrate on scientific reasoning.

This seemingly simple difference has enormous consequences.

Scientists become more productive.

Code becomes easier to understand.

Collaboration improves.

Errors decrease.

Research becomes more reproducible.

Innovation accelerates.

One might ask whether concise syntax sacrifices computational sophistication.

The answer is absolutely not.

Behind that single command lies a highly optimized computational engine.

The user sees simplicity.

The computer performs complexity.

Good software design hides unnecessary complexity while exposing conceptual clarity.

This principle appears repeatedly throughout R.

Whether estimating regression models,

Performing principal component analysis,

Generating bootstrap samples,

Computing confidence intervals,

Training neural networks,

Performing Bayesian inference,

Constructing survival models,

Running Monte Carlo simulations,

Estimating generalized linear mixed models,

or implementing deep learning architectures,

the syntax consistently reflects statistical thinking rather than computational mechanics.

Another remarkable strength of R syntax lies in its object-oriented philosophy.

Everything in R is an object.

Vectors.

Matrices.

Lists.

Data frames.

Functions.

Models.

Plots.

Results.

Everything.

This consistency creates remarkable flexibility.

When a researcher fits a regression model, the result is not merely a printed table.

It becomes an object containing coefficients, residuals, fitted values, confidence intervals, variance estimates, diagnostics, significance tests, prediction methods, visualization tools, and numerous additional components.

The model itself becomes a living computational object that can be explored, manipulated, updated, compared, validated, and extended.

This design philosophy greatly simplifies algorithm development.

Complex computational procedures can be decomposed into smaller reusable components.

Functions receive objects.

Functions produce objects.

Objects interact with other objects.

Entire computational pipelines become modular.

This modularity is one reason why R packages integrate so seamlessly with one another.

A visualization package can immediately plot results generated by another modelling package.

A machine learning package can utilize preprocessing functions developed elsewhere.

A Bayesian package can interface with simulation libraries.

The entire ecosystem behaves like a coordinated scientific community.

Equally important is R's functional programming paradigm.

Many programming languages revolve around procedures.

R revolves around functions.

Functions are treated as first-class citizens.

Functions can receive other functions as inputs.

Functions can return functions.

Functions can generate new functions.

This flexibility becomes extraordinarily powerful in computational statistics.

Imagine developing an optimization algorithm.

Instead of rewriting the optimization procedure for every mathematical problem, one simply supplies a different objective function.

The optimization engine remains unchanged.

Only the mathematical expression changes.

This separation between computational machinery and statistical theory creates highly reusable algorithms.

The same optimization framework can estimate regression coefficients today, Bayesian posterior distributions tomorrow, and neural network parameters next week.

Only the statistical objective changes.

The computational architecture remains intact.

Such elegance reflects exceptional language design.

Another characteristic that distinguishes R syntax is vectorization.

Many newcomers initially overlook this feature.

Experienced statisticians quickly recognize its importance.

Vectorization allows operations to be applied simultaneously across entire datasets.

Instead of processing observations individually, R processes collections of observations together.

This approach produces several advantages.

Programs become shorter.

Execution becomes faster.

Memory usage becomes more efficient.

Algorithms become easier to understand.

Statistical notation becomes more closely aligned with mathematical notation.

Indeed, one of the greatest compliments that can be paid to R syntax is that it often resembles the equations appearing in statistical textbooks.

This similarity reduces the gap between mathematical theory and computational implementation.

Students learn concepts more quickly.

Researchers make fewer translation errors.

Published algorithms become easier to reproduce.

Scientific communication improves.

Another defining characteristic of R is its extraordinary graphics capability.

Visualization is not merely decoration.

Visualization is computation.

Many important discoveries occur because researchers observe unexpected patterns.

Outliers.

Clusters.

Nonlinear relationships.

Interactions.

Temporal changes.

Spatial structures.

Distributional characteristics.

Visualization transforms numerical information into scientific insight.

R's graphics philosophy reflects this understanding.

Instead of producing static figures merely for publication, R encourages exploratory graphics.

Researchers interact with data visually throughout the analytical process.

Plots become thinking tools rather than presentation tools.

This mindset aligns perfectly with computational algorithmic statistics, where exploration often precedes formal modelling.

As technology evolved, so too did R.

Support for parallel computing emerged.

Interfaces with C++, Python, Java, SQL, and cloud computing platforms expanded.

Machine learning libraries flourished.

Deep learning frameworks became available.

High-performance computing improved dramatically.

Big data integration matured.

Yet despite these technological advances, the fundamental philosophy of R remained unchanged.

Express statistical reasoning clearly.

Hide unnecessary computational complexity.

Promote reproducibility.

Encourage openness.

Support collaboration.

Enable discovery.

These principles explain why R continues to thrive despite increasing competition from numerous programming languages.

The language has never attempted to become everything for everyone.

Instead, it has remained exceptionally good at what it was originally designed to accomplish.

Scientific computing.

Statistical reasoning.

Computational discovery.

As educators, we often remind students that programming languages evolve.

Technologies change.

Software platforms emerge and disappear.

However, elegant design principles endure.

The strength of R syntax does not depend upon current technological trends.

It rests upon timeless principles of scientific thinking.

Those principles remain just as relevant today as they were when R was first introduced more than three decades ago.

Understanding this historical journey allows us to appreciate an important reality.

The remarkable success of R was never an accident.

Its syntax was carefully shaped by statisticians for statisticians.

Its architecture reflects decades of accumulated scientific wisdom.

Its continued relevance demonstrates that when software truly understands the needs of its users, it transcends technology and becomes an indispensable instrument of discovery.
